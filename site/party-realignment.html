How America's Political Parties Changed

A simple way to follow how American party coalitions changed from the Whig era to the modern two-party system.

The names stayed longer than the coalitions did.

American party organizations have long histories, but the voters, regions, factions, and ideas associated with them have changed substantially over time.

2 The old party system

Before Republicans: Democrats and Whigs

By the 1830s, the main national competition was between Andrew Jackson's Democrats and the Whigs.

Jacksonian Democrats

  • More suspicious of federal economic activism
  • More emphasis on state and local authority
  • Opposed the national bank
  • Generally favored lower tariffs
  • Strong agrarian and populist identity

Whigs

  • More willing to use federal power for national development
  • Favored stronger national economic institutions
  • Supported a national bank
  • Often favored protective tariffs
  • Strong commercial and developmental identity
Context

These older party divisions do not map neatly onto today's Democratic and Republican coalitions. The issues and political categories of the 1830s need to be understood in their own historical setting.

3 The system breaks

Slavery breaks the old party system

By the 1850s, the question of whether slavery would expand into new western territories became harder for the old parties to contain.

The Whigs included Northern members increasingly opposed to slavery's expansion and Southern members representing slaveholding states. Democrats split too.

1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act

The slavery question is reopened in western territories, accelerating the collapse of the Whigs.

1856 Republicans run nationally

The new party centers opposition to slavery's expansion.

1860 Democrats split

Northern and Southern Democratic factions divide sharply over slavery in the territories.

The Democratic Party continued from the Jacksonian coalition. The Whig Party fragmented, and many former Whigs—including Abraham Lincoln—helped build the Republican Party.

4 The new coalition

The early Republican coalition

The early Republican Party was built primarily around stopping the extension of slavery into the territories.

Its 1856 and 1860 platforms made that clear. But the party also carried forward recognizable Whig economic ideas such as protective tariffs, infrastructure, western development, and support for a transcontinental railroad.

Coalition structure

The original Republican coalition combined anti-slavery politics with a national-development economic program inherited partly from the Whigs.

5 Civil War and after

Emancipation, abolition, and Reconstruction

Lincoln was a Republican, and the Republican Party became the principal national political force behind emancipation, abolition, and Reconstruction.

Republicans supplied most of the Senate votes for the Thirteenth Amendment. After the war, congressional Republicans drove measures that produced the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments and federal civil-rights legislation.

Historical scope

In compressed form, it is accurate to describe Republicans as the principal party behind abolition during the Civil War era.

Comparing that party with later Republican coalitions requires tracing the political changes that occurred afterward.

To answer that second question, we have to keep following the coalition.

6 Mid-century coalitions

Both parties contained broad ideological wings

For much of the twentieth century, knowing someone's party did not tell you their ideology as reliably as it often does now.

Democrats included

Northern liberals labor-oriented New Dealers moderates Southern conservatives segregationists

Republicans included

progressives Northeastern liberals moderates business conservatives Western conservatives

Conservative Republicans often cooperated with conservative Southern Democrats against parts of the New Deal and Fair Deal.

Mental model

Party and ideology were overlapping circles, not the same circle.

7 Another realignment

The New Deal moves Black voters

After the Civil War, Black voters had powerful reasons to identify with the Republican Party: Lincoln, emancipation, Reconstruction, and the Reconstruction Amendments.

During Franklin Roosevelt's presidency, the New Deal transformed the federal government's economic role. Its programs were often discriminatory, but they also delivered meaningful relief and employment to many Black families. Democratic organizations increasingly courted Black voters.

1934 Arthur Mitchell elected

The first Black Democrat is elected to Congress.

1936 Black voting shifts sharply

A majority of Black voters back Roosevelt's coalition.

Late 1940s Black voters are consistently Democratic

This shift was already underway before the major civil-rights battles of the 1960s.

8 Civil-rights division

1948: civil rights divides the Democratic coalition

The Democratic coalition now contained Northern liberals, Black voters, labor, urban voters—and a powerful Southern segregationist wing.

In 1948, the national Democratic platform adopted a stronger civil-rights plank. A group of Southern delegates broke with the national party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party, the Dixiecrats.

National Democrats

Move toward stronger federal civil-rights commitments.

Dixiecrats

Break away and explicitly defend racial segregation.

This did not immediately transform the entire Democratic Party. It showed that the national party's growing civil-rights commitments were becoming increasingly difficult to reconcile with its segregationist Southern wing.

9 The hinge years

1960 and 1964: sorting is underway

In 1960, both national parties endorsed substantial civil-rights action in their platforms.

Four years later, the Civil Rights Act exposed the old coalition structure. Southern senators filibustered the bill. Democratic supporters needed Republican votes to break that filibuster.

At the same time, conservative Barry Goldwater won the Republican nomination and carried five Deep South states plus Arizona in the general election.

Historical significance

1964 was a major turning point within a longer realignment rather than a complete party transformation by itself.

10 Slow realignment

The Southern realignment unfolded over decades

Presidential voting, congressional voting, state and local offices, and individual party affiliation did not all change at the same pace. A conservative Southern Democrat elected in 1950 might remain a Democrat until retirement, while a younger conservative seeking the same office decades later might run as a Republican.

11 Cause and context

Race, civil rights, and other forces

Research using Gallup data from 1958 onward finds strong evidence that racially conservative Southern White defection was central to the decline of Democratic identification in the South.

That makes it difficult to treat civil rights as incidental to the realignment.

Broader context

The modern coalitions that emerged over the following decades were also shaped by economics, religion, geography, party organization, cultural issues, and other changes.

The evidence supports treating race and civil rights as a central axis of the Southern realignment within a larger, decades-long restructuring of American politics.

12 The modern shape

The factions sort

Over time, conservative Democrats became less common. Liberal and moderate Republicans became less common. The parties became more internally consistent.

Mid-century

Both parties contain meaningful liberal, moderate, and conservative wings.

Later

Democrats become more consistently center-left/liberal; Republicans more consistently center-right/conservative.

This is ideological sorting. It is different from a literal swap.

13 Common shorthand

Useful shorthand and its limits

“Republicans freed the slaves.”

What it describes accurately: Republicans were the principal political force behind Lincoln, emancipation, abolition, and Reconstruction.

What it does not explain by itself: more than a century of later coalition and ideological change.

“Democrats were the party of segregation.”

What it describes accurately: Southern Democratic organizations and politicians were the major defenders of Jim Crow for decades.

What it does not explain by itself: Northern liberal Democrats, Black voters' New Deal-era movement toward Democrats, the 1948 Dixiecrat revolt, and the later collapse of the old Southern Democratic system.

“The parties switched.”

What it describes accurately: major voter blocs, regions, and ideological factions eventually aligned very differently.

What it does not explain by itself: the process took generations and different things moved at different times.

“The party switch is a myth.”

What it describes accurately: a one-day, one-for-one exchange never happened.

What it does not explain by itself: documented changes in Black voting, Southern White voting, regional strength, party platforms, factional control, and ideological overlap.

Party organization

This is the formal organization and name: for example, the Democratic Party or Republican Party.

The organization can survive for generations even when the voters, factions, and policy priorities inside it change.

Think: the sign over the building.

Coalition

The coalition is the collection of voters, regions, social groups, economic interests, organizations, and movements that support a party.

Coalitions can change dramatically while the party name stays the same.

Think: who lives inside the building.

Faction

A faction is a wing inside a party. Mid-twentieth-century America had liberal Republicans, conservative Republicans, liberal Democrats, and conservative Democrats.

Which faction controls a party can change even if the party organization remains continuous.

Think: different groups arguing over what the building should become.

Ideology

Ideology means the actual political ideas a person or faction holds.

A historical party label alone does not establish ideology; the person's positions on the issues of their own time provide the stronger evidence.

Think: what the people inside believe and want government to do.

Quick glossary

Realignment

A major change in which voters, regions, or groups support which party.

Ideological sorting

The process by which liberals and conservatives become more consistently concentrated in different parties.

Coalition

The voters and groups supporting a party.

Faction

A competing wing inside a party.

Dixiecrats

The States' Rights Democratic Party formed in 1948 by segregationist Southern Democrats who broke with the national Democratic civil-rights plank.

Solid South

The long period when the former Confederate South was overwhelmingly Democratic in national and state politics.

What the 1856–1860 platforms show

Republicans

The 1856 Republican platform argued that Congress had the power and duty to prohibit slavery in the territories. The 1860 platform again opposed slavery's extension while also supporting tariffs, infrastructure, western settlement, and a Pacific railroad.

Democrats

The Democrats fractured. The Southern/Breckinridge faction demanded federal protection for slaveholding property claims in the territories and defended enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Law.

Takeaway: slavery's expansion was breaking apart the older party system, while the new Republican coalition also carried older Whig economic ideas.

The 1964 coalition

The Civil Rights Act did not pass as a simple Democrats-versus-Republicans vote.

The Senate filibuster came from the Southern bloc. Democratic civil-rights supporters needed Republican votes to end it, and Republican leader Everett Dirksen played a major role in assembling those votes.

Cloture coalition:

44 Democrats + 27 Republicans = 71 votes to end the filibuster.

Both the 1964 Democratic and Republican national platforms supported implementation of the new Civil Rights Act.

Takeaway: 1964 was a major hinge year within a longer process of ideological sorting.
