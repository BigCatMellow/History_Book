# The Names Stayed. The Coalitions Changed.

> **Big idea:** American political parties are organizations that contain coalitions of voters and competing ideological factions. The organization can keep the same name even while the people, regions, and ideas inside it change.

---

# The Question

Abraham Lincoln was a Republican.

Southern segregationists were Democrats.

Today, the Republican Party is the major conservative party and the Democratic Party is the major liberal/left-of-center party.

So what happened?

Did the parties simply switch places?

**No.**

But they also did not remain ideologically unchanged.

The better model is:

> **The party names survived while the coalitions inside them repeatedly changed and eventually became much more ideologically sorted.**

That process took generations.

---

# The Simple Story

For most of American history, a party name did **not** tell you someone's ideology as reliably as it often does today.

There were:

- conservative Democrats;
- liberal Democrats;
- conservative Republicans;
- liberal Republicans.

There were also enormous regional differences.

A Democrat from Mississippi could have very different politics from a Democrat from Minnesota.

A Republican from New York could have very different politics from a Republican from Arizona.

Over time, those mismatched factions gradually sorted themselves.

The easiest way to understand the change is:

```text
PARTY NAME
stays relatively stable

but inside it...

VOTERS
REGIONS
FACTIONS
IDEOLOGY

can change
```

That is the basic story.

Now we can add the history.

---

# Before Republicans: Democrats and Whigs

In the 1820s and 1830s, American politics reorganized around **Andrew Jackson's Democrats** and their main opponents, the **Whigs**.

The U.S. Senate's historical office describes Jackson's Democratic coalition as favoring more state authority and a smaller federal role, while the opposing National Republican/Whig tradition favored policies such as a national bank and federal spending on roads, canals, and bridges.

The two parties also fought over tariffs and other questions of national economic development.

So, in very simplified form:

| Democrats | Whigs |
| --- | --- |
| more suspicious of federal economic power | more willing to use federal power for national development |
| stronger emphasis on states/local authority | stronger national institutions |
| hostile to the national bank | supportive of a national bank |
| more agrarian/populist identity | more commercial/developmental identity |

That does **not** mean:

> Democrats were today's Republicans and Whigs were today's Democrats.

The issues, economy, electorate, and meaning of political labels were too different for that.

It simply means that the early parties were organized around political arguments that do not line up neatly with the modern party divide.

**Source:** [U.S. Senate, “About Parties and Leadership — Historical Overview”](https://www.senate.gov/about/parties-leadership/overview.htm)

---

# Then slavery breaks the old party system

The Whigs had a structural problem.

They were a national party containing both:

- Northern politicians increasingly hostile to the expansion of slavery;
- Southern politicians who lived in and represented slaveholding states.

The Democrats had sectional divisions too.

For a while, both parties could keep these disagreements inside their coalitions.

Then the country kept expanding westward.

That created a question that became harder and harder to avoid:

> **Would slavery be allowed to expand into new American territories?**

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 intensified the crisis by allowing settlers in those territories to decide the slavery question through “popular sovereignty.”

The Whig coalition fractured.

A new coalition formed from groups including:

- former Whigs;
- anti-slavery and Free Soil activists;
- Northern Democrats opposed to slavery's expansion.

That coalition became the **Republican Party**.

This is an important correction:

> **The Whigs did not become the Democrats. Many former Whigs helped create the Republican Party.**

Abraham Lincoln himself had been a Whig. As the slavery-expansion crisis intensified, he moved into the new Republican coalition.

The new party's central unifying position was initially **opposition to the expansion of slavery into the western territories**.

# So yes: Republicans really did lead the fight that ended slavery

This part should not be softened.

During the Civil War, Lincoln was a Republican president.

Republicans became the major congressional force for permanent abolition.

When the Senate passed the Thirteenth Amendment in 1864, the successful coalition included **30 Republicans**, along with several Democrats and Unionists.

After the war, Republicans in Congress led Reconstruction measures that:

- established national citizenship;
- expanded federal protection of civil rights;
- produced the Fourteenth Amendment;
- produced the Fifteenth Amendment;
- attempted to protect the political rights of formerly enslaved Americans.

So:

> **“Republicans freed the slaves” is a broadly accurate statement about the party of the Civil War and Reconstruction era.**

Where the argument goes wrong is if someone adds:

> “Therefore the ideological coalitions called Republican and Democratic today must be essentially the same coalitions that existed in 1865.”

That conclusion does not follow automatically.

It skips the next century of American political history.

# The part that is hardest for modern readers: both parties later contained left and right wings

After the Civil War, American politics did not immediately turn into:

```text
Democrats = left
Republicans = right
```

For much of the twentieth century, that would have been a poor description.

Both parties had internal ideological wings.

You could find:

```text
DEMOCRATS

Northern liberals
labor-oriented New Dealers
urban political machines
Southern conservatives
segregationists
moderates
```

and:

```text
REPUBLICANS

progressives
Northeastern liberals/moderates
business conservatives
Midwestern conservatives
Western conservatives
```

That meant ideology sometimes crossed party lines.

For example, in the late 1930s and 1940s, conservative Republicans often cooperated with conservative Southern Democrats against parts of Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal and Harry Truman's Fair Deal.

The Senate's historical office describes Republican leader Robert Taft as commanding a coalition of **conservative Republicans and Southern Democrats** against Truman's domestic agenda.

That sentence alone tells us something important:

> Party and ideology were not the same thing.

[U.S. Senate, Charles McNary](https://www.senate.gov/art-artifacts/fine-art/paintings/32_00012_000.htm)  
[U.S. Senate, Robert A. Taft](https://www.senate.gov/about/parties-leadership/taft-a-robert.htm)

# The New Deal begins another major realignment

For generations after the Civil War, Black Americans had a strong historical attachment to the Republican Party—the party of Lincoln, emancipation, and Reconstruction.

That began changing dramatically during Franklin Roosevelt's presidency.

Roosevelt's **New Deal** greatly expanded the federal government's economic role during the Great Depression.

New Deal programs were discriminatory in important ways and did not dismantle Southern segregation.

But they also delivered meaningful federal economic assistance, and Roosevelt's broader coalition attracted:

- workers;
- labor unions;
- urban immigrants;
- farmers;
- progressives;
- increasing numbers of Black voters.

The Library of Congress notes that by **1936**, a majority of Black voters had abandoned their historic Republican allegiance and joined Roosevelt's coalition.

The House historian notes that Black voters were voting consistently Democratic by the late 1940s.

This was a major political realignment.

But it created a strange Democratic coalition.

The Democratic Party now contained both:

```text
Northern liberals
        +
Black voters increasingly
        +
labor
        +
urban voters
        +
Southern white conservatives
        +
segregationist Southern politicians
```

That coalition could win elections.

But on civil rights, its members increasingly wanted incompatible things.

**Sources:**  
[Library of Congress, “The Segregation Era”](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/civil-rights-act/segregation-era.html)  
[U.S. House, “Party Realignment and the New Deal”](https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/BAIC/Historical-Essays/Keeping-the-Faith/Party-Realignment--New-Deal/)

# Civil rights exposes the fault lines

This is where the usual “party switch” story becomes especially misleading.

Consider the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

If the parties had already become today's ideological coalitions, we might expect a simple:

```text
Democrats vs. Republicans
```

fight.

That is not what happened.

The major Senate filibuster came from the **Southern bloc**, dominated by conservative Southern Democrats.

But Democratic civil-rights supporters could not break that filibuster by themselves.

They needed Republican votes.

Republican leader Everett Dirksen helped negotiate the bill and assemble enough Republican support for cloture.

The final cloture vote was:

```text
44 Democrats
+
27 Republicans
=
71 votes to end the filibuster
```

That is the old party system in one picture.

The more useful dividing line was often:

```text
civil-rights liberals/moderates
        vs.
Southern segregationists and other opponents
```

not simply:

```text
Democrat
vs.
Republican
```

**Source:**  
[U.S. Senate, “Cloture and Final Passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964”](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/landmark-legislation/civil-rights-act-of-1964/cloture-and-final-passage-of-the-civil-rights-act-of-1964-essay.htm)

# 1964 is important—but it is not the day the parties switched

The Republican Party was also changing internally.

In 1964, conservative Senator **Barry Goldwater** defeated the more moderate/liberal Eastern Republican establishment represented by figures such as Nelson Rockefeller.

Goldwater lost the general election badly.

But he carried **five Deep South states**.

That was striking because the Deep South had historically been one of the strongest Democratic regions in the country.

Goldwater's campaign became an important marker of conservative ascendancy inside the Republican Party and foreshadowed later Republican strength in the South.

But saying:

> **“The parties switched in 1964.”**

still gets the history wrong.

Most Southern Democratic politicians did not suddenly become Republicans.

Many stayed Democrats for years or decades.

State and local Democratic power survived longer than presidential Democratic dominance in much of the South.

What changed was often generational.

A conservative Southern politician elected as a Democrat in 1955 might remain a Democrat for his entire career.

A younger conservative politician seeking the same seat decades later might run as a Republican.

Voters could also change gradually:

```text
Democratic for everything
        ↓
Republican for president,
Democratic locally
        ↓
Republican for president and Senate
        ↓
increasingly Republican down-ballot
```

That is realignment without every politician physically switching parties.

The House historian describes Southern White voters beginning in the 1960s a **decades-long transformation** toward Republican allegiance.

That phrase—**decades-long transformation**—is much more useful than “the parties switched.”

# Meanwhile, the factions disappear

Think of the parties as two boxes containing mixed pieces.

Around the mid-twentieth century:

```text
DEMOCRATIC BOX
liberals
moderates
conservatives
Southern segregationists

REPUBLICAN BOX
liberals
moderates
conservatives
```

Over the following decades, those pieces increasingly sorted.

Conservative Democrats became less central and eventually much rarer.

Liberal Republicans became less central and eventually much rarer.

The Republican Party became increasingly identified with the conservative movement.

The Democratic Party became increasingly identified with liberal and later progressive politics.

By the early twenty-first century, party identity and ideological identity overlapped much more strongly than they had during the mid-twentieth century.

Pew Research Center's long-running values surveys illustrate the later stage of that sorting. In its 2017 analysis, Pew found far less ideological overlap between Democratic and Republican voters than it had measured in 1994.

That does not mean every Democrat is liberal or every Republican is conservative.

It means those labels now predict ideology **much more reliably** than they once did.

**Source:**  
[Pew Research Center, “Partisan divides over political values widen”](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2017/10/05/1-partisan-divides-over-political-values-widen/)

# Look Closer: did Democrats and Republicans simply trade beliefs?

No.

This is the second major misconception.

A long political realignment is not the same thing as exchanging identical boxes of beliefs.

Political issues themselves change.

For example, the Whigs favored:

- national economic development;
- infrastructure;
- tariffs;
- commercial expansion;
- national banking.

Some of those positions have analogues in modern Democratic policy.

Others have analogues in modern Republican policy.

Likewise, Jacksonian Democrats combined:

- suspicion of concentrated financial power;
- states' rights;
- populist attacks on elites;
- a more limited federal economic role.

Pieces of that tradition can also be found in different parts of modern politics.

So the historical family tree is not:

```text
OLD DEMOCRATS
↓
MODERN REPUBLICANS

OLD REPUBLICANS
↓
MODERN DEMOCRATS
```

It is closer to:

```text
older political traditions
        ↓
slavery / Civil War
        ↓
industrialization
        ↓
Progressivism
        ↓
New Deal
        ↓
civil rights
        ↓
conservative movement
        ↓
regional + demographic change
        ↓
cultural/economic change
        ↓
modern party coalitions
```

Ideas, voters, institutions, and factions get recombined along the way.

# What the “party switch” story gets right

The phrase survives because it describes something people can clearly see.

Some enormous political relationships really did reverse.

For example:

### Black voters

```text
Reconstruction era
strongly Republican
        ↓
New Deal era
major movement Democratic
        ↓
later twentieth century
core Democratic constituency
```

### White Southern voters

```text
historically
strongly Democratic
        ↓
mid-twentieth century onward
increasing Republican presidential voting
        ↓
later decades
Republican strength spreads further down the ballot
```

### Ideological wings

```text
mid-20th century:
liberal Republicans + conservative Democrats common

later:
both become much rarer
```

So if someone says:

> “The party coalitions changed enormously.”

that is supported by the historical record.

If someone says:

> “Every Democrat became a Republican and every Republican became a Democrat in 1964.”

that is not.

---

# What the “party switch is a myth” story gets right

It also points to real problems with the simplified story.

It is true that:

- Republicans really did lead abolition;
- Republicans really did lead Reconstruction;
- Republicans really did provide essential votes for the Civil Rights Act of 1964;
- most segregationist Democrats did not immediately change their party registration;
- the South remained Democratic in many state and local offices long after Republican presidential breakthroughs;
- not every political issue reversed.

Those facts matter.

But they do not establish that the parties remained ideologically and electorally unchanged.

A better explanation has to fit **both sets of evidence**.

That is why “realignment and ideological sorting” is more useful than either slogan.

# How This Became Modern America

The most important modern legacy is not that one party secretly “is” the other.

It is that Americans now live in a political system where **party label and ideology are much more tightly connected than they were for much of the country's history**.

That makes it easy to project modern categories backward.

We see:

> Republican

and unconsciously hear:

> conservative.

We see:

> Democrat

and unconsciously hear:

> liberal.

That shortcut works much better today than it does when reading about 1835, 1865, 1935, or even 1964.

So when reading American political history, separate four things:

```text
1. PARTY ORGANIZATION
What institution/name?

2. COALITION
Which voters, regions, economic interests, and social groups?

3. FACTIONS
Which ideological wings are competing inside the party?

4. IDEOLOGY
What does this specific person/group actually believe?
```

Then the apparent contradiction disappears.

Lincoln can genuinely have been a Republican.

Republicans can genuinely have led abolition and Reconstruction.

Southern segregationists can genuinely have been Democrats.

Republicans can genuinely have helped pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Black voters can genuinely have moved strongly Democratic.

Southern White conservatives can genuinely have moved strongly Republican.

And modern Democrats and Republicans can genuinely represent ideological coalitions very different from the parties of 1865.

All of those statements can be true at the same time.

---

# The model to remember

Do not imagine two teams trading jerseys.

Imagine two old houses whose **names stay over the doors while generations of residents move in and out, rooms are rebuilt, and the purpose of each house changes**.

The addresses remain recognizable.

The people living inside do not.

Or, even shorter:

> **The names stayed. The coalitions changed.**

---

# Transfer: Can You Test the Claim?

Consider this argument:

> “Lincoln was a Republican. Lincoln opposed slavery. Therefore modern Republicans have the same political ideology as Lincoln, while modern Democrats inherit the ideology of Lincoln's Democratic opponents.”

What is wrong with the reasoning?

A strong answer should notice that the argument:

1. correctly identifies Lincoln's party;
2. correctly recognizes the Republican Party's central role in emancipation;
3. then assumes that a party organization's **name** proves two centuries of ideological continuity;
4. ignores later changes in voters, factions, regions, and policy;
5. therefore needs additional historical evidence before it can connect Lincoln's coalition directly to a modern one.

Now reverse it:

> “Southern segregationists were Democrats, therefore modern Democrats have the same ideology as segregationist Southern Democrats.”

The same reasoning failure appears.

The historical label is real.

The assumed ideological continuity is what has to be demonstrated.
